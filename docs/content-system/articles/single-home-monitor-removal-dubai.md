The item is one computer monitor from a desk at home. It is a screen, it has a stand and a few leads, and it does not go in a waste sack. Unplug it, decide which leads go with it, keep the face of the screen safe, and book it as one screen. That is the whole job, and the details below are where it usually goes wrong.

This note is one monitor. Electronics in general, and where each kind can be taken, are the [e-waste guide](/blog/e-waste-disposal-dubai). A television is the [TV disposal guide](/blog/tv-disposal-dubai), even when it was used as a computer screen. Screens, towers and servers leaving an office are the [IT equipment guide](/blog/it-equipment-disposal-dubai). A home printer and a box of old leads are the [printer-and-cables note](/blog/home-printer-and-cables-dubai). Come back here when the question is one monitor from a home desk.

## Make sure it is a monitor

A monitor, for this note, is a screen that shows what a separate computer sends it. It has a power input, one or more video inputs, and usually a stand. It does not run programs by itself.

Look at the back. Video sockets such as HDMI, DisplayPort, USB-C or an older blue VGA plug mean it takes a signal from somewhere else. Write what you can see: “HDMI and DisplayPort on the back, one power lead”. If you cannot read the labels, write “sockets not read”.

An all-in-one computer looks like a monitor and is not. The computer is inside the screen. It may have a keyboard and mouse that connect without a tower, a disc slot on the side, or a label on the back with a computer model name. An all-in-one holds files. It is a computer, and it is not this note. Name it as a computer and use the e-waste guide or, for a business, the IT equipment guide, where the question of the drive is covered.

A laptop is not a monitor, even when it sits closed on a stand beside one. A tablet in a stand is not a monitor. A television with a remote and a tuner is a television. If you used a television as your desk screen, it is still a television on the booking. Write “television, used as a monitor” and follow the TV guide.

If you are not sure, write the model name from the label on the back and say “not sure whether all-in-one”. That is a better line than guessing either way.

## One screen, not the desk

The monitor is usually the only electronic item a desk seems to have, until you look under it. The tower, the speakers, the webcam on top of the screen, the docking station, a USB hub, the keyboard, the mouse, a desk lamp with its own plug, and a power strip are all on the same desk. They are not the monitor.

Decide what is leaving. If only the monitor is leaving, the other things stay, and they stay connected or put away, not piled beside the screen. If some of them are leaving too, they are separate lines. “One monitor. One pair of desk speakers. One keyboard.” Three lines, not “computer stuff”.

A webcam clipped to the top of the monitor is its own small item. Unclip it. If it is leaving, write “webcam, leaving, unclipped”. If it is staying, put it with the laptop or the tower it belongs to. A webcam that goes out still clipped to a screen you are getting rid of is a webcam you lose.

A soundbar or light bar mounted on the monitor is the same. Take it off if it lifts or unclips by hand. Write what it is.

If the desk itself is leaving, it is a furniture job and not this note. The [standing-desk note](/blog/home-standing-desk-removal-dubai) covers one sit-stand desk at home. The monitor can be a line on the same visit. It is still a screen on that list, kept apart from the desk frame.

## Unplug it and decide which leads go

Switch the monitor off at the button, then at the wall. Unplug the power lead from the wall first, then from the back of the monitor. Unplug the video lead from the computer end, then from the monitor end.

Now decide about each lead. This is the part people skip.

The power lead belongs to this monitor. If it is a plain lead with a plug at one end, it can go with the screen. Some monitors use a separate power brick, a small block on the cable. That brick belongs to this monitor too. Keep it with the screen. Write “power brick, with monitor” or “power lead, with monitor”.

The video lead may belong to the computer. A USB-C cable that also charges your laptop is not spare because the monitor is leaving. An HDMI lead you will use with the next screen is not spare. Decide for each one. Write “HDMI lead leaving with monitor” or “HDMI lead kept”.

If you are not keeping any leads, coil them loosely and tie them with the lead’s own strap or a piece of string. Put them in a small bag and tape the bag to the back of the monitor, away from the screen face. Do not wrap a lead tightly around the stand, and do not tape a lead across the face.

A drawer of old leads from earlier screens and earlier computers is a different list. That is the printer-and-cables note, which deals with a box of mixed leads. Do not empty that drawer into this monitor’s bag.

## The stand, the arm, and what is fixed

Most monitors stand on a foot. Some sit on an arm that clamps to the desk or screws through it. Some hang on a bracket on the wall. Write which.

A plain foot often comes off. Many stands release with a button on the back, or with a screw you can turn with your fingers or a coin. If the stand comes off by hand, and you already know how it releases, you may take it off. Lay the monitor face down on a soft surface first, a folded towel or the bed, not the desk edge. Write “stand off, by hand, with monitor”. Keep the stand and any screw in the same bag.

If the stand does not release by hand, leave it on. A monitor that leaves standing on its own foot is fine. Write “stand left on”.

A desk arm is a different object. The monitor screws onto a plate on the arm, and the arm clamps to the desk. Decide whether the arm is leaving. If the arm stays on the desk, the monitor has to come off the plate. If the plate screws are ones you already turn by hand or with a screwdriver you already use on them, you may take the screen off. Support it with one hand while the last screw comes out. Write “monitor off arm, arm staying” or “arm leaving, clamp released by hand”. If the clamp needs a tool you do not have, leave it on the desk and write “arm still clamped”. Do not lever a clamp off the desk edge.

A monitor on a wall bracket is fixed to the wall. In a rented home, the bracket and the holes are part of the wall question. Write “wall bracket”. Lift the monitor off the bracket only if it lifts off by hand and you already know it does. Otherwise leave it. This note does not decide who may take a bracket down or fill the holes. The person who owns the wall decides that.

Do not pull a monitor off an arm or a bracket by its edges while the screws are still in. A screen takes the strain through the frame, and the frame is where it cracks.

## Protect the face of the screen

The front of the monitor is the part you cannot fix. A scratch or a pressure mark on the panel is permanent. A cracked panel can leave sharp edges and small fragments.

Do not lay anything on the screen. Do not lean the monitor face down on a hard floor, on tiles, or against the corner of a table. Do not stack books, a keyboard or a box on top of it while it waits.

If you have the original box and the foam inserts, and they still fit, you may pack the monitor back into them. Write “in original box”. If you do not, stand the monitor upright, face toward a wall, with a towel or a folded sheet between the screen and the wall. Write “standing, screen to wall, towel on face”. Do not wrap a screen tightly in plastic film. Film that presses on a panel can leave marks, and it hides cracks from the person collecting it.

If the screen is already cracked, write “cracked” and say where: “crack across the lower left corner”. Do not tape over a crack to hide it. If small pieces have fallen, sweep them up with a brush and pan, put them in a rigid container, and write “loose fragments, in a box”. Keep them with the monitor. Do not put glass fragments in a soft bag.

A curved monitor is easier to damage than a flat one when it is laid down. Do not lay a curved screen face down at all. Stand it on its foot or upright against a wall with the curve facing the wall and a towel between.

## Keep the screen out of the waste sacks

A monitor does not go in a black sack with kitchen rubbish. It does not go in a bag of offcuts, tiles or packaging from a room you are refitting. It does not go down a chute, and it does not get left by the bin room to see who takes it.

If the home is being renovated, and the monitor is in the same room as the bags, lift it out of that pile. If the bags are also leaving as a project load, [keep the screen out of the waste sacks](/services/waste-removal) and say in the message that the monitor is a separate line from the sacks. That page is the project load. The monitor is not part of it because it happens to be in the same room.

Where a screen can be taken, and which official options exist, is already set out in the e-waste guide. This note does not repeat a list of drop-off points, and it does not say what a particular centre accepts. Those details change. Read that guide if you are deciding between dropping a screen off yourself and having it collected.

If someone wants the monitor and it works, arrange that handover yourself. Keep the screen out of the leaving photos until they have it. A message that says “I might take it” is not a handover. Until they collect it, the monitor is still in the room and still on your list.

The business removes items. It does not buy them, sell them or say what a monitor is worth.

## A second screen is a second line

Many home desks have two monitors, or a spare one in a cupboard from an earlier setup. If two are leaving, write two lines. Give each its size, its stand and its condition. “Monitor one: flat, stand on, working. Monitor two: curved, stand off, crack on the right edge.”

Do not write “monitors” with no number. Do not stack two screens face to face to save space while they wait. Stand them separately, each with its own towel.

If there are three or more, or if the screens are part of a wider clear-out of electronics, the e-waste guide is the better place to plan the whole group. This note stays the single screen. It can still be used for each monitor in that group, one at a time, so each one is described properly.

If the screens are from a business, a home office used for a company, or equipment the company owns, ask the company first. Equipment that belongs to an employer is not yours to send away because it is in your flat. The IT equipment guide covers screens that leave an office.

## Measure it, or say you did not

The size printed on a monitor is the diagonal of the screen, not the width of the object. Measure the monitor itself: width across the frame, height with the stand on or off, and depth at the deepest point, which is usually the stand or the bulge at the back.

If you have no tape, write the screen size from the label and “not measured”. Do not invent the other numbers. This note does not keep a standard size for a monitor.

A single monitor will pass most doors. The point of the size is the vehicle and the carry, and whether it can travel upright. Say whether it is boxed, upright with a towel, or stand off and flat in a box.

Weight is not something to guess. A large monitor can be heavier than its slim front suggests. Write “heavy for its size” if that is what you found when you lifted it. Do not write a number you did not weigh.

## Photos of the screen, not the room

![An older flat-screen television on a console in a bright, generic apartment living room.](media:tv-disposal-cover)

The picture is a flat-screen television on a console in an apartment living room. It is not a computer monitor, and it does not show a stand release, a desk arm, a power brick or a cracked panel. Use it only as a reminder that a screen should stand upright with nothing leaning on it. Your own photos still have to show the monitor, unplugged, with its leads.

Send these views.

- The front of the monitor, switched off, so the size and the frame can be seen.
- The back, so the sockets, the stand and any bracket or arm can be seen.
- The label on the back, close enough to read the model, if you can.
- Any crack, scratch or pressure mark on the screen, in good light.
- The leads and the power brick that are leaving with it, laid beside it.
- Anything else on the desk that is leaving, as its own item, or a note that nothing else is.

Add the room and the floor. Add a lift or loading note if you have already been given one. If you have not, write “no lift instruction yet”. Do not invent a lift size, and do not write a building rule you have not been given.

## One line in the booking

Say, in one message:

- one computer monitor, flat or curved, and the screen size from the label
- not an all-in-one, or “not sure”, with the model name
- unplugged, with the power lead or brick, and which video leads go with it
- stand on, stand off by hand, off a desk arm, or on a wall bracket
- working, not working, or not tested
- any crack, scratch or loose fragments, and where
- boxed, or standing upright with the screen protected
- anything else from the desk that is leaving, as separate lines

Do not write “electronics” as the only word. Do not write “small item, can go in a bag”. Do not put the monitor in a sack because the sacks are already going. One screen is one line.

If the monitor turns out to be an all-in-one computer, stop and move it to the e-waste guide. If it turns out to be a television, move it to the TV guide. The leads you decided to keep stay with the computer that uses them.
